How reading works

Your eyes don’t glide across the page. They leap.

Reading feels like one smooth motion. Up close it is a rapid string of jumps and pauses — and almost all of the work happens while your eyes stand still.

Essay · 2 min read

Watch a friend read and you will notice it straight away: their eyes do not slide along a line the way a fingertip might. They hop. Each hop is a saccade, a movement so quick that it is over in a few hundredths of a second, and so frequent that an ordinary page of prose can take several hundred of them.

Between the hops come the pauses, and that is where reading actually happens. These pauses are called fixations, and a typical one lasts about a quarter of a second. In that moment the sharp centre of your vision takes in roughly one word, while the blurrier edge picks up clues about the next: how long it is, and the shape of its first few letters.

What happens mid-jump

Your brain is not idle during a saccade, but it is not reading either. Vision is largely switched off mid-jump, which is why the room never seems to smear sideways when you glance across it. Add up all those tiny blackouts and a surprising slice of reading time is spent travelling rather than seeing.

Roughly one jump in eight goes backwards. These regressions are how we repair a misread word, or a sentence that took an unexpected turn. Rapid serial visual presentation — showing words one at a time in a single spot — takes the travelling out of the picture: the text comes to your eyes, so they can stay put on the letter that matters.
